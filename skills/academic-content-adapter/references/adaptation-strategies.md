# Adaptation strategies by learning profile

Each section covers what tends to help, what to actively avoid, and a short before/after example. These are starting points, not rigid rules — real students are individuals, and a user who tells you "this specific thing works better for my kid" should always override the general guidance here.

## Dyslexia

**Do:**
- Short sentences, one clause where possible. Break compound sentences apart.
- Avoid italics for anything long — italics are harder to decode. Bold is fine for emphasis.
- Sans-serif fonts in documents (Arial, Verdana, Calibri, Comic Sans is also commonly recommended for this specific profile despite its reputation elsewhere).
- Left-align, never justify. Justified text creates uneven gaps ("rivers" of whitespace) that break up word shapes.
- Chunk text into short paragraphs (3-4 lines max) with visible whitespace between them.
- Spell out numbers under 10 or use digits consistently — pick one convention and stick to it within a document, since inconsistency adds decoding load.
- When introducing a hard/technical word, keep it (don't hide the vocabulary the student needs to learn) but pair it with a plain-language gloss the first time it appears.

**Avoid:**
- Long, unbroken paragraphs.
- Dense text with many multi-syllable words in a row, even if individually each word is fine.
- Relying on subtle letter differences to disambiguate meaning (e.g., word pairs that are easy to visually confuse) without context clues.

**Example:**
- Before: "A fotossíntese é o processo pelo qual as plantas, utilizando a luz solar, a água absorvida pelas raízes e o dióxido de carbono presente no ar, conseguem produzir seu próprio alimento, liberando oxigênio como subproduto desse processo."
- After: "Fotossíntese é como a planta faz sua própria comida. Ela usa três coisas: luz do sol, água e ar. No final, ela solta oxigênio no ar — o oxigênio que a gente respira."

## ADHD

**Do:**
- Break tasks into small, clearly numbered steps — a student can lose the thread partway through a long instruction.
- Put the most important information first, not buried in the middle of a paragraph.
- Use checklists and boxes to check off — externalizing progress helps with task-switching and working memory.
- Keep exercises short with natural stopping points, rather than one long uninterrupted worksheet.
- Bold or highlight the actual action word in each instruction ("**Circle** the verbs", "**Write** one sentence").
- Minimize visual clutter — decorative borders, unrelated images, or busy layouts compete for attention.

**Avoid:**
- Multi-part instructions in a single sentence ("Read the text, then answer the questions, and don't forget to write in complete sentences").
- Long stretches of text with no visual break or checkpoint.
- Open-ended tasks with no clear stopping point or success criterion.

**Example:**
- Before: "Leia o texto com atenção, responda as quatro perguntas abaixo com frases completas e depois desenhe uma cena que representa a história."
- After a numbered breakdown:
  1. **Leia** o texto.
  2. **Responda** as perguntas 1 a 4 (frases completas).
  3. **Desenhe** uma cena da história.
  - [ ] Marquei cada passo que já fiz.

## Autism (ASD)

**Do:**
- Use literal, precise language. Say exactly what you mean.
- Keep structure predictable — if a worksheet uses a pattern (question, then blank line, then next question), keep that pattern consistent throughout rather than varying it.
- Make expectations explicit: what counts as "done," how long an answer should be, what format is expected.
- Concrete examples over abstract descriptions.

**Avoid:**
- Idioms, sarcasm, and figurative language ("quebrar a cabeça", "não é nenhum bicho de sete cabeças") unless the exercise is specifically teaching figurative language — in which case, flag it explicitly as figurative rather than letting it blend into literal instructions.
- Vague quantifiers ("escreva um pouco sobre...") — replace with something concrete ("escreva de 2 a 4 frases sobre...").
- Sudden shifts in format or expectation partway through a document without a clear heading marking the change.

**Example:**
- Before: "Não tenha medo de errar, é moleza!"
- After: "Você pode errar enquanto está aprendendo. Isso é normal."

## Mild intellectual disability / general processing difficulty

**Do:**
- Very short, concrete sentences. Avoid abstraction — ground ideas in examples the student can picture.
- Heavy repetition of key terms and ideas across the material, rather than stating something once and moving on.
- Visual supports (simple diagrams, icons next to instructions) alongside text, not as a replacement for it.
- Slow, explicit step-by-step sequencing, even more granular than the ADHD breakdown above.
- Reduce the total amount of content per page/session — it's often better to cover less material more thoroughly than to compress everything into one dense adaptation.

**Avoid:**
- Multi-step reasoning presented in one block ("if X then Y, unless Z, in which case...").
- Abstract or hypothetical framing ("imagine que...") without a concrete anchor.
- Assuming background knowledge that hasn't been explicitly taught in the material itself.

## Dyscalculia (math-specific difficulty)

**Do:**
- Pair numerals with concrete visual representations (groups of objects, number lines) wherever the format allows it.
- Break multi-step word problems into separate numbered operations, and label what each step is finding.
- Keep consistent spatial layout for the same operation type (e.g., always stack subtraction the same way) — inconsistent layout adds cognitive load on top of the math itself.
- State units and what a number means explicitly rather than relying on the student to infer it from context.

**Avoid:**
- Word problems that bury the needed numbers inside long narrative sentences.
- Mixing multiple operations in a single unlabeled step.

## General reading/comprehension difficulty (no specific diagnosis given)

When the user doesn't specify a profile, combine the broadly-applicable techniques from the sections above rather than picking one at random:
- Short sentences and paragraphs (dyslexia + ADHD overlap)
- Numbered, single-action steps (ADHD + intellectual disability overlap)
- A glossary for hard terms (dyslexia)
- Concrete examples over abstraction (autism + intellectual disability overlap)

This combination is a safe, effective default and rarely hurts a student who has a diagnosis you weren't told about.
