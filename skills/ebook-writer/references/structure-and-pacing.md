# Structure and pacing

## Standard shape for a 30-35 page ebook

| Section | Approx. words | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Title page | — | Title, subtitle, author (formatting skill handles layout) |
| Table of contents | — | Generated from chapter headings |
| Introduction | 400-600 | Hook + what the book delivers + who it's for |
| Chapters (8-12) | 600-1,000 each | One core idea per chapter |
| Conclusion | 400-600 | Synthesize, don't just summarize — leave the reader with the single thing to remember |
| Call-to-action (optional) | 100-200 | Only if the book's goal (Stage 1) calls for one |

Total body: roughly 8,000-10,000 words, which renders to about 30-35 pages at typical ebook formatting (≈275 words/page, 11-12pt body text, standard margins). Actual page count depends on the formatting skill's output — treat the word count as the design target and verify real page count after formatting (see SKILL.md Stage 6).

## Ordering chapters

Two patterns cover most non-fiction ebooks:

- **Problem → framework → application.** Early chapters establish the problem and why it matters, middle chapters build the core idea/framework, late chapters show how to apply it. Good for advice/how-to books.
- **Progressive build.** Each chapter depends on the previous one, building toward a capability the reader couldn't do at chapter 1. Good for skill-based or step-by-step books.

Whichever pattern fits, put the highest-uncertainty chapter (the one most likely to need rework, usually the core idea/framework) earlier rather than later, so any needed course-correction happens before too much has been written on top of it.

## Chapter internal shape

A chapter that's easy to follow (and easy to stop and resume, which matters for a "keep reading" feel across sessions) tends to follow:

1. **Hook** (see `references/engagement-techniques.md`)
2. **Set up why this matters to the reader specifically**
3. **Deliver the core idea**, broken into digestible sub-points, each grounded in an example or made concrete with an analogy where needed
4. **Address the obvious objection or edge case** — anticipating "but what about..." builds trust that the author has actually thought this through
5. **Close with the takeaway** — what should the reader do or remember, stated plainly, not buried in the last sentence of a long paragraph

Not every chapter needs all five beats explicitly, but most benefit from hitting them in roughly this order.

## Pacing signals to watch for while drafting

- If a chapter is running long relative to its budget, check whether it's actually two ideas that should split into two chapters, rather than padding it into one oversized one.
- If a chapter is running short, check whether it's missing an example/story rather than needing generic filler added.
- If several consecutive chapters open the same way (e.g., all start with a rhetorical question), vary it — see engagement-techniques.md.
