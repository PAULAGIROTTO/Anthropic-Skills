# Research and fact-checking

The trust a reader places in a book is fragile — one obviously made-up statistic and they stop believing everything else in it, including the parts that were true. This is the highest-leverage place to spend care in the whole workflow.

## Where claims can come from

In order of preference:

1. **The user's own expertise or material.** If they've done the thing the book describes (ran the business, coached the clients, lived the experience), their firsthand account is a legitimate source — ask them directly rather than searching for a study to back up something they already know from experience. Attribute it as their experience/observation, not as universal fact.
2. **A source you actually looked up this session.** Web search or fetch, then read what it actually says — not what the search snippet implies it says. Note the source (publication, org, year) so it can be cited or at least stands behind the claim if the user asks "where did that come from?"
3. **Well-established, uncontested general knowledge** that doesn't need a citation to be credible (e.g., "water boils at 100°C at sea level"). Use judgment on where the line is — if you'd hedge it in conversation with an expert, hedge it here too.

What does **not** belong in the book: a specific number, percentage, study result, or named claim recalled from memory without checking it this session. Language models produce fluent, specific-sounding numbers that are simply wrong at a meaningfully high rate — that's not a risk worth taking in a document meant to be trustworthy. If a claim would strengthen the book but can't be verified, either look it up, ask the user if they have a source, or rewrite the sentence to not depend on it (e.g., "many people report X" instead of "73% of people experience X").

## Fact vs. opinion vs. framework

Non-fiction books mix three kinds of content, and readers trust a book more, not less, when it's honest about which is which:

- **Fact** — verifiable and checked. State plainly.
- **Opinion / recommendation** — the author's (or user's) judgment call. Signal it as such ("in my experience...", "a good starting point is...") rather than dressing it up as settled fact.
- **Framework / model** — a simplified way of thinking about something (e.g., "the four types of X"). Useful and often the most memorable part of a book, but should be presented as a lens, not as scientific taxonomy, unless it actually is one.

Blurring these — stating a framework as if it were proven fact — is a common way AI-written content ends up sounding authoritative about things that are actually just organizing devices.

## Handling numbers and statistics specifically

- Prefer ranges and attributed sources over precise-sounding unsourced numbers ("estimates suggest 60-70%, according to [source]" beats a bare "68%").
- If a statistic is central to an argument, it's worth a real search even if it slows things down — this is exactly the kind of claim a skeptical reader fact-checks.
- Dates, version numbers, prices, and named studies age fast. Note the year/context so the claim doesn't silently go stale, and flag anything genuinely from a fast-moving field (technology, regulation, pricing) as time-sensitive.

## When you can't verify something

Don't just quietly drop it and hope the gap isn't noticed, and don't quietly leave it in and hope it's right — tell the user. A short note like "I couldn't confirm the exact adoption numbers for X — I've reframed that paragraph without a specific figure, let me know if you have a source" takes one sentence and preserves trust in the rest of the process.
