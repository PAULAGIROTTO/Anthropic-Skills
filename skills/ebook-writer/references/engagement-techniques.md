# Engagement techniques

"Addictive" non-fiction isn't about tricks — it's about removing every small bit of friction that gives a reader a reason to put the book down, and giving them a small reason to keep going instead. These techniques work together; not every chapter needs all of them.

## Opening hooks (first 1-3 sentences of intro and each chapter)

A chapter that opens with "In this chapter, we will discuss..." tells the reader nothing they can't get from the table of contents, and reads as a reason to skim. Open instead with one of:

- **A concrete scene or story** — a specific person, moment, or situation (real, or a realistic composite clearly framed as illustrative) that puts the reader inside the problem before explaining it abstractly.
- **A surprising fact or reframe** — something that contradicts what the reader probably assumes, immediately followed by "here's why that's true" (only use facts that passed the fact-check stage).
- **A sharp question** — one the reader will recognize themselves in, not a rhetorical throwaway. "Why does saying 'no' to a client feel harder than the client's actual demand?" beats "Have you ever struggled with boundaries?"
- **A small stakes statement** — what does getting this chapter's idea wrong actually cost the reader? Naming the cost early gives them a reason to keep reading for the fix.

Avoid starting two chapters in a row with the same hook type — the pattern itself becomes predictable and stops working.

## Storytelling and examples

Abstract advice is forgettable; a specific example is not. Where possible, ground a chapter's core idea in:

- A real example (from the user's material, research, or common well-known cases) — most credible.
- A clearly-labeled illustrative/composite scenario ("imagine someone who...") when a real one isn't available — still useful, but don't present it as if it happened.

Return to the same example or character across a chapter (or even across the book) rather than introducing a new one for every point — recurring examples build the light narrative pull that makes a book feel like it's going somewhere, not a checklist.

## Rhythm and light suspense

- Vary sentence length. A short sentence after a few longer ones lands harder. Use it deliberately at turns in the argument, not every other line.
- End a section with a light forward pull when it's earned — a question the next section answers, or a consequence explored next — but never a manufactured cliffhanger that oversells what's actually coming. Readers notice and resent the bait.
- Use sub-headers within longer chapters (past ~600 words) so the page doesn't look like a monolithic block — visual chunking alone reduces the feeling of a slog.

## Making complex ideas easy to understand

- **Analogy before explanation.** Introduce an unfamiliar concept by comparing it to something the reader already understands, then layer in the precise detail. The analogy doesn't need to be perfect — it needs to build a first foothold.
- **One idea per paragraph.** If a paragraph is doing two jobs, split it.
- **Define jargon the first time, then never make the reader wonder again.** If a term must be used repeatedly, define it once clearly and simply, ideally with a mini-example.
- **Prefer the concrete word.** "Cut your invoice-to-payment time from 30 days to 10" beats "significantly improve cash flow velocity."

## What to avoid

- Exaggerated claims or manufactured urgency ("this ONE trick," "everything you know is wrong") — these read as untrustworthy at exactly the moment the book is trying to earn trust, and undercut the fact-checking work from Stage 2.
- Padding a chapter to hit a word count with restated points — a reader feels padding even when they can't name it, and it's the fastest way to make a book feel like it's not respecting their time.
- Cramming every technique into every paragraph. A chapter that hooks, tells a story, drops an analogy, varies rhythm, and ends on suspense all within 700 words reads as try-hard. Pick what the content actually calls for.
